<p>You don't need a craft room or a trip to the pet store to keep your dog entertained. All five of these use stuff you probably already have — old t-shirts, socks, tennis balls, a cardboard box. None of them take more than 10-15 minutes to make.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> the braided t-shirt tug toy is the easiest and fastest — no tools, no sewing, just braiding. Start there if you want a quick win.</p>
<div style="background: #FBF3EC; border: 1px solid #E8C4A8; border-radius: 10px; padding: 16px 20px; margin: 24px 0; font-size: 15px; line-height: 1.6;">
<strong style="color: #8A4A2E;">A safety note:</strong> DIY toys need more supervision than store-bought ones — check them regularly for fraying, loose threads, or small pieces that could come loose, and retire anything that's breaking down.
        </div>
<h2 id="tshirt-tug" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">1. Braided T-Shirt Tug Toy</h2>
<p>Cut an old t-shirt into 3 long, even strips. Tie a knot at one end to anchor them, then braid the three strips together like hair, tying off the other end when you're done. Great for tug-of-war and pretty durable for a fabric toy.</p>
<h2 id="sock-ball" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">2. Sock &amp; Tennis Ball Toy</h2>
<p>Drop a tennis ball into an old sock and tie a knot right above the ball. This adds bounce and unpredictability to fetch, and the sock gives smaller dogs something softer to grip than a bare ball.</p>
<h2 id="fleece-nosew" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">3. No-Sew Fleece Tug Toy</h2>
<p>Cut two rectangles of fleece fabric (about 6x20 inches), stack them, and cut matching fringe strips about 1 inch wide along both long edges — leave the middle uncut. Tie each pair of fringe strips together (front piece to back piece) all the way down. You'll end up with a sturdy, chewable tug toy with zero sewing involved.</p>
<h2 id="cardboard-puzzle" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">4. Cardboard Puzzle Box</h2>
<p>Cut small flaps into the sides of an empty cardboard box (like a cereal box), hide a few treats or kibble inside, and fold the flaps most of the way closed. Your dog has to figure out how to open the flaps or tear through to get the treats. Always supervise cardboard play — you don't want them actually eating the cardboard.</p>
<h2 id="bottle-dispenser" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">5. DIY Treat-Dispensing Bottle</h2>
<p>Take an empty plastic bottle, remove the label and cap, and cut a few holes slightly larger than your dog's kibble around the sides. Fill with kibble and let your dog roll it around to shake pieces out. Best for supervised play only, and skip this one for dogs who tend to chew through plastic.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Are DIY toys as durable as store-bought ones?</strong><br/>Generally no — they're great for supervised play and occasional use, but a dedicated power chewer will go through fabric toys faster than a rubber one built for it.</p>
<p><strong>How do I know when to throw a DIY toy away?</strong><br/>Once you see fraying threads, holes big enough for stuffing or pieces to come out, or any part your dog could swallow, it's time to retire it.</p>
<p><strong>Can I use any old fabric?</strong><br/>Cotton t-shirts and fleece both work well. Avoid anything with buttons, zippers, or decorative pieces that could come off and become a choking hazard.</p>
