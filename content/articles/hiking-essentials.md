<p>Hiking with a dog is one of the best parts of having one, but it's easy to underestimate what they actually need out there. A dog can't tell you they're overheating or that their pads are getting torn up, so a little prep goes a long way. Here's what's actually worth packing.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> water (for both of you), a properly fitted harness and leash, poop bags, a basic first-aid kit, and ID on their collar are the non-negotiables. Everything else below just makes the trip smoother.</p>
<h2 id="water" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Water &amp; Hydration</h2>
<p>Dogs can overheat faster than people, especially on warmer trails. Bring more water than you think you need, plus a collapsible bowl. A general rule of thumb is about 8 ounces of water per hour of moderate activity for a medium-sized dog, more in the heat.</p>
<h2 id="safety" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Safety Gear</h2>
<ul style="margin: 14px 0; padding-left: 22px; line-height: 1.9;">
<li><strong>A well-fitted harness</strong> — more secure and comfortable than a collar for pulling on uneven terrain.</li>
<li><strong>A 6-foot (non-retractable) leash</strong> — better control near wildlife or other hikers.</li>
<li><strong>Up-to-date ID tags and a microchip</strong> — trails are exactly where dogs slip leashes and get spooked.</li>
<li><strong>A bright collar or bandana</strong> — helps visibility if you're hiking during hunting season or at dawn/dusk.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="firstaid" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">First-Aid Basics</h2>
<p>A small dog-specific kit (or a few key items added to your own) covers most trail mishaps: gauze and vet wrap, tweezers for splinters or ticks, antiseptic wipes, a tick removal tool, and a muzzle (even the calmest dog may bite when they're in pain). Know the nearest emergency vet's number before you head out somewhere remote.</p>
<h2 id="food" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Food &amp; Energy</h2>
<p>Pack a few high-value treats or a small portion of their regular food for longer hikes — dogs burn through calories fast on the trail. Our <a class="cphd-inlinelink" href="/pb-banana-cups/">homemade treat recipes</a> travel well if you want something more exciting than plain kibble.</p>
<h2 id="comfort" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Comfort &amp; Weather</h2>
<p>Check paw pads before, during, and after the hike for cuts or hot spots — booties help on rocky or hot terrain. In cold weather, a fitted jacket helps short-haired or small dogs conserve heat. And always check the trail's dog policy and any seasonal leash rules before you go.</p>
<h2 id="etiquette" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Trail Etiquette</h2>
<p>Pack out every bit of waste (even the biodegradable bags), keep your dog leashed unless it's a designated off-leash trail, and give hikers, horses, and wildlife a wide berth. A little courtesy keeps trails dog-friendly for everyone.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How do I know if a trail is too much for my dog?</strong><br/>Start shorter and easier than you think, especially for puppies, seniors, or brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds who overheat more easily. Watch for excessive panting, lagging behind, or reluctance to keep moving.</p>
<p><strong>Do dogs need boots on the trail?</strong><br/>Not always, but they help a lot on hot pavement, sharp rocky terrain, or snow and ice — anywhere paw pads are at real risk.</p>
<p><strong>What's the single most forgotten item?</strong><br/>Water for the dog, specifically — people remember their own but assume the dog will "be fine," which is how overheating happens.</p>
