faq:
- question: Does my dog's sleep position mean anything about their personality?
  answer: Loosely — dogs who sleep sprawled out and exposed tend to be more relaxed by nature, while cautious or new-to-the-home dogs often curl up tighter until they settle in. It's a tendency, not a rulebook.
- question: Should I worry if my dog suddenly changes sleep positions?
  answer: A single night, no. A consistent, lasting change — especially toward curling up tighter, restlessness, or avoiding a usual comfortable position — is worth mentioning at their next vet visit, since it can sometimes reflect discomfort.
- question: Why does my dog sleep upside down?
  answer: Usually just comfort and cooling off — it's actually one of the most trusting positions a dog can sleep in.
- question: Do sleep positions change with age?
  answer: Often, yes. Senior dogs and dogs with joint stiffness tend to shift toward curled-up or side-lying positions that put less pressure on their hips and joints.

<div style="font-size: 12px; font-weight: 700; letter-spacing: 0.06em; color: #B4552F; margin-bottom: 6px;">QUICK ANSWER</div>
<p style="margin: 0; font-size: 16px; line-height: 1.6;">Sleep position mostly comes down to comfort and temperature — but it can also hint at how safe and relaxed your dog feels. Curled up tight usually means conserving warmth; sprawled out belly-up almost always means "I feel completely safe here."</p>
</div>
<p>I used to think my dog just flopped down wherever gravity took her. Then I started paying attention — she's a curl-into-a-donut sleeper 90% of the time, but the second she's fully relaxed (usually after a long walk, in a sunbeam, with zero reason to be alert), she goes full Superman, front legs stretched out like she's about to fly off the couch. Turns out that shift actually means something.</p>
<h2 id="curled-up" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">The Curled-Up Ball (Donut)</h2>
<p>Nose tucked near the tail, body wrapped into a tight circle — this is the most common sleep position for a reason. It's instinctual: curling up conserves body heat and protects vital organs, a holdover from a dog's wild ancestors sleeping exposed outdoors. A dog who sleeps this way most nights isn't necessarily anxious — it's just comfortable, efficient sleep. It becomes worth noting only if a normally sprawled-out dog suddenly starts curling up tight and staying that way, which can sometimes point to feeling cold, unwell, or a little on edge.</p>
<h2 id="side-sleeper" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">The Side Sleeper</h2>
<p>Legs stretched out, body fully on one side, often mid-snore. This is about as relaxed as a dog gets — full muscle relaxation, easy breathing, and a body language expert's clearest sign of complete comfort and trust in their surroundings. If your dog does this often, especially somewhere central like the living room floor, take it as a compliment: they feel completely safe there.</p>
<h2 id="superman" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">The Superman (Belly Down, Legs Out)</h2>
<p>Flat on the belly, front legs stretched forward, back legs kicked out behind — like they're about to launch off the ground. This one's popular with puppies and high-energy dogs because it doubles as a "ready" position: cool on the belly, quick to spring up if something interesting happens. It's less about deep sleep and more about a quick power nap between zoomies.</p>
<h2 id="back-to-back" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Back-Against-You (Or Another Dog)</h2>
<p>Some dogs sleep pressed right up against a person or another pet — spine to spine, or curled into your legs. This is a bonding position more than anything: body contact releases oxytocin for both of you, and it's a strong sign your dog considers you (or their housemate) part of their safe inner circle.</p>
<h2 id="belly-up" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Belly-Up ("Dead Bug")</h2>
<p>Flat on the back, all four legs in the air, belly fully exposed — the single most vulnerable position a dog can sleep in, since it exposes the throat and stomach. That's exactly why it's the clearest sign of total relaxation and trust. Bonus practical upside: it also helps dogs cool off, since the belly has less fur and more exposed skin to release heat.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Does my dog's sleep position mean anything about their personality?</strong><br/>Loosely — dogs who sleep sprawled out and exposed tend to be more relaxed by nature, while cautious or new-to-the-home dogs often curl up tighter until they settle in. It's a tendency, not a rulebook.</p>
<p><strong>Should I worry if my dog suddenly changes sleep positions?</strong><br/>A single night, no. A consistent, lasting change — especially toward curling up tighter, restlessness, or avoiding a usual comfortable position — is worth mentioning at their next vet visit, since it can sometimes reflect discomfort.</p>
<p><strong>Why does my dog sleep upside down?</strong><br/>Usually just comfort and cooling off — it's actually one of the most trusting positions a dog can sleep in.</p>
<p><strong>Do sleep positions change with age?</strong><br/>Often, yes. Senior dogs and dogs with joint stiffness tend to shift toward curled-up or side-lying positions that put less pressure on their hips and joints.</p>
<p style="margin-top: 32px;">Next time you catch your dog mid-nap, take a second look at the shape they've made themselves into — it's a pretty reliable little window into how safe, warm, and comfortable they feel in that exact moment. Mine's currently doing the full Superman on the dog bed six feet away. Living her best life.</p>
