<div style="font-size: 12px; font-weight: 700; letter-spacing: 0.06em; color: #B4552F; margin-bottom: 6px;">QUICK ANSWER</div>
<p style="margin: 0; font-size: 16px; line-height: 1.6;">That corn-chip smell comes from naturally occurring bacteria and yeast (mainly <em>Proteus</em>, <em>Pseudomonas</em>, and <em>Malassezia</em>) living on your dog's paws. It's harmless in most dogs and just part of having warm, damp, hairy feet — though redness, swelling, or a much stronger smell than usual is worth a vet visit.</p>
</div>
<p>I noticed it for the first time during a normal evening cuddle session — I picked up my dog's paw to trim a nail and got hit with a smell that was, unmistakably, Fritos. Not "kind of like" Fritos. Exactly like Fritos. I panicked a little, Googled it at 11pm like a responsible pet parent, and found out I am extremely not alone. It's such a common thing that vets have a nickname for it: "Frito feet."</p>
<h2 id="whats-causing-it" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">What's Actually Causing the Smell</h2>
<p>Your dog's paws are warm, a little damp, and covered in fur that traps moisture — basically a perfect little ecosystem for bacteria and yeast that live on skin naturally. The usual suspects are <em>Proteus</em> and <em>Pseudomonas</em> bacteria, along with <em>Malassezia</em> yeast, and as they go about their business, they give off a smell that happens to land somewhere between corn chips and popcorn. It's the exact same chemistry that makes some people's feet smell a certain way after a long day in sneakers — dogs just get a snack-food version of it.</p>
<h2 id="why-some-stronger" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Why Some Dogs Smell Stronger Than Others</h2>
<p>A few things make Frito feet more noticeable: heavier paw fur that traps more moisture, humid climates, dogs who spend a lot of time outside or in water, and dogs with naturally oilier skin. Breeds with thick paw fur or webbed feet tend to be repeat offenders. It's not about cleanliness — plenty of very well-groomed dogs still have it, because it's mostly a matter of paw anatomy and the bacteria that naturally live there.</p>
<h2 id="worry" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Is It Something to Worry About?</h2>
<p>Usually, no — a mild corn-chip smell on its own is just biology. What's worth a vet visit is when the smell shows up alongside other signs: redness, swelling, limping, constant licking or chewing at the paw, discharge, or a smell that's suddenly much stronger or more "off" than usual. Those can point to a yeast or bacterial overgrowth, an infection, or something stuck between the toes — all easily treatable, but worth checking rather than guessing at home.</p>
<h2 id="reduce-smell" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">How to Reduce the Smell (If You Want To)</h2>
<p>You don't have to do anything — it's not a health problem in most dogs. But if it bothers you, a few easy habits help:</p>
<ul style="margin: 14px 0; padding-left: 22px; line-height: 1.9;">
<li><strong>Wipe paws after walks</strong> with a damp cloth or a vet-approved paw wipe to cut down on trapped moisture and debris.</li>
<li><strong>Trim the fur between paw pads</strong> so air can actually reach the skin instead of staying locked in.</li>
<li><strong>Dry paws thoroughly</strong> after baths or rainy walks — damp fur between the toes is where the smell thrives.</li>
<li><strong>Keep nails trimmed</strong> so toes aren't splayed in a way that traps extra moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Check between the toes weekly</strong> for early signs of irritation, debris, or matting.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Do all dogs get Frito feet?</strong><br/>No, but it's extremely common — enough that it has its own nickname among vets and dog owners.</p>
<p><strong>Is Frito feet contagious to other dogs or people?</strong><br/>No. It's just the ordinary bacteria and yeast that naturally live on your dog's own skin.</p>
<p><strong>Can I use human foot powder or deodorant on my dog's paws?</strong><br/>Skip it — those products aren't formulated for dogs and can irritate the skin. Stick to paw wipes or products made for pets.</p>
<p><strong>Does the smell mean my dog needs a bath?</strong><br/>Not necessarily — a full bath won't fix it long-term since the bacteria come right back. Regular paw wipes and drying matter more than frequency of baths.</p>
<p><strong>Why does it smell stronger in summer?</strong><br/>Heat and humidity mean more sweat and moisture on the paws, which gives the bacteria and yeast more to work with.</p>
<p style="margin-top: 32px;">Go smell your dog's paws tonight — you now have full permission, and honestly, once you know what's causing it, it's a genuinely fun little party trick to spring on friends who don't believe you. Corn chips, every time.</p>
