  answer: Provide enough toys that resource guarding has less to compete over, supervise play closely at first, and separate them immediately at the first sign of stiffening or guarding rather than waiting for an actual fight.

  answer: Slow, neutral-territory introductions on leash work best — avoid tight spaces and high-value items like food or toys during the first meetings.

<p>Multiple dogs means multiplying almost everything — but it doesn't have to double the chaos. A little structure around feeding, walking, and individual attention keeps the household running smoothly.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> feed dogs in separate spots to avoid resource guarding, rotate who gets walked first, and carve out at least a few minutes of one-on-one time with each dog daily so nobody's needs get lost in the group.</p>
<h2 id="feeding" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Feeding: Separate Spaces</h2>
<p>Even dogs who get along fine can tense up around food. Feeding in separate rooms or at a distance removes the opportunity for resource guarding to become a habit, regardless of how well they normally get along.</p>
<h2 id="walks" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Walks: Together or Separate</h2>
<p>Walking dogs together works well once leash manners are solid, but a new dog, a reactive one, or a big size mismatch usually does better with separate walks, at least at first. Rotate who gets the earlier or longer walk so it doesn't always favor one dog.</p>
<h2 id="one-on-one" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">One-on-One Time Matters</h2>
<p>It's easy for individual dogs to blend into "the dogs" as a unit. A few minutes of solo attention — a short training session, a car ride, or just calm petting without the others around — keeps each dog's bond with you distinct.</p>
<h2 id="training" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Training Individually First</h2>
<p>New skills stick better when taught one dog at a time before practicing as a group — dogs are easily distracted by each other, and one dog's mistakes can become a habit the others pick up.</p>
<h2 id="downtime" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Managing Shared Downtime</h2>
<p>Multiple dogs means multiple personalities sharing a couch. Separate beds or crates give each dog a space that's unambiguously theirs, which reduces tension during rest time.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How do I stop my dogs from fighting over toys?</strong><br/>Provide enough toys that resource guarding has less to compete over, supervise play closely at first, and separate them immediately at the first sign of stiffening or guarding rather than waiting for an actual fight.</p>
<p><strong>Should new dogs be introduced together?</strong><br/>Slow, neutral-territory introductions on leash work best — avoid tight spaces and high-value items like food or toys during the first meetings.</p>
<p><strong>Is it harder to train multiple dogs?</strong><br/>It takes more structure, not necessarily more difficulty — training separately before group practice and being consistent with cues across all dogs makes it manageable.</p>
