<p>Working from home with a dog sounds like the dream — and mostly, it is. But dogs who never experience being alone can struggle badly the one time you do have to leave, whether that's an errand, a trip, or eventually a return to an office. A good routine builds in both togetherness and independence.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> structure your day around work blocks and dog blocks, and deliberately practice short periods of separation — even while you're home — so being alone never becomes a big deal.</p>
<h2 id="morning" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Morning: Exercise Before You Log On</h2>
<p>A walk or play session before your first meeting burns off morning energy so your dog is more likely to settle during your first work block instead of pawing at you 20 minutes in. This is also a good time for breakfast, timed so they're not begging at your desk right as you start.</p>
<h2 id="work-blocks" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">During Work Blocks: Practice Being Apart</h2>
<p>It's tempting to let your dog shadow you everywhere, but that can quietly build dependence on your constant presence. Give them a comfortable spot in a different room during at least one work block a day, with a long-lasting chew or a <a class="cphd-inlinelink" href="/kong-fillers/">stuffed Kong</a> — this teaches them that you being out of sight is normal and fine, not something to panic about.</p>
<h2 id="breaks" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Breaks: Short and Genuinely Theirs</h2>
<p>Use natural breaks between meetings for a quick potty trip, a few minutes of tug, or a short training refresh. Five focused minutes of real attention beats an hour of distracted co-presence where you're half-watching a screen the whole time.</p>
<h2 id="lunch" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Lunch: The Longer Reset</h2>
<p>A midday walk, even a short one, breaks up the day for both of you and helps prevent the restless afternoon energy spike. This is also a good window to leave the house briefly — a grocery run or short errand — to build in real practice being alone that isn't just "in another room."</p>
<h2 id="evening" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">End of Day: Close the Laptop, Actually Close It</h2>
<p>When work ends, make the transition to dog time obvious — a walk, dinner, or play session that clearly signals the workday is over. Dogs pick up on these transitions fast, and a clear "on/off" pattern helps them settle during work blocks because they know full attention is coming later.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Is it bad for my dog to never be alone?</strong><br/>It can set them up for a rough time if your situation ever changes — a new job, travel, or even a full day of errands. Practicing short separations regularly, even while you're home, keeps that skill from atrophying.</p>
<p><strong>My dog cries when I close the door to a separate room. What now?</strong><br/>Start smaller — a baby gate instead of a closed door, or just a few minutes at first — and pair it with something high-value like a stuffed Kong, building up the duration gradually.</p>
<p><strong>How do I stop my dog from interrupting video calls?</strong><br/>A pre-call walk to burn energy, plus a long-lasting chew given right as you sit down, handles most of it. Consistency in doing this every time helps them learn what "the call face" means.</p>
<p style="margin-top: 32px;">The goal isn't less time with your dog — it's making sure some of that time builds real independence, so a day apart down the road doesn't come as a shock to either of you.</p>
