faq:
- question: My rescue dog seems shut down and won't eat much at first. Is that normal?
  answer: Some appetite and energy changes in the first few days are common as a dog adjusts. If it continues beyond a few days or they seem unwell, check in with your vet.
- question: How long until my rescue dog acts like themselves?
  answer: It varies widely, but many people notice a real shift around the 3-week mark, with fuller settling-in over the following months. Every dog's timeline is different.
- question: Should I take them to the dog park right away?
  answer: It's best to wait — new dogs benefit from a quieter introduction to their new life before adding the intensity of an off-leash dog park.

<p>A newly adopted rescue dog is adjusting to an entirely new world — new smells, new people, new rules — often with an unknown history behind them. A predictable, low-pressure routine is the fastest way to build trust.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> keep the first couple of weeks quiet and predictable, introduce new people and places gradually, and let your dog set the pace rather than rushing socialization or training in the first days home.</p>
<h2 id="decompression" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">The First Few Days: Decompression</h2>
<p>Many rescue organizations recommend a "3-3-3" mindset — 3 days to decompress, 3 weeks to learn the routine, 3 months to truly settle in. Keep the first days quiet: minimal visitors, short low-key walks, and lots of downtime.</p>
<h2 id="predictable" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Build a Predictable Schedule Early</h2>
<p>Consistent meal times, walk times, and bedtime help a rescue dog learn what to expect, which reduces anxiety faster than almost anything else. Write the schedule down if it helps everyone in the house stay consistent.</p>
<h2 id="space" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Give Them a Safe Space</h2>
<p>A crate, a bed in a quiet corner, or a gated-off room gives a nervous dog somewhere to retreat that's entirely theirs. Let them choose to come out and explore rather than pulling them into new situations.</p>
<h2 id="socializing" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Introduce New Things Gradually</h2>
<p>Hold off on the dog park, big gatherings, or new houseguests for at least the first couple of weeks. Let your dog build confidence in their new home before adding more novelty on top.</p>
<h2 id="training" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Start Training Gently</h2>
<p>Basic cues and house rules can start early, but keep sessions short and heavily reward-based — a rescue dog is still learning that this home is safe, and training should build confidence, not add pressure.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>My rescue dog seems shut down and won't eat much at first. Is that normal?</strong><br/>Some appetite and energy changes in the first few days are common as a dog adjusts. If it continues beyond a few days or they seem unwell, check in with your vet.</p>
<p><strong>How long until my rescue dog acts like themselves?</strong><br/>It varies widely, but many people notice a real shift around the 3-week mark, with fuller settling-in over the following months. Every dog's timeline is different.</p>
<p><strong>Should I take them to the dog park right away?</strong><br/>It's best to wait — new dogs benefit from a quieter introduction to their new life before adding the intensity of an off-leash dog park.</p>
