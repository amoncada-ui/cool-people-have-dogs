<p>Bringing home your first dog is exciting — and it's easy to either overbuy at the pet store or forget something basic in the process. Here's what you actually need, split into what matters on day one versus what can wait.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> at minimum you need food and water bowls, a collar with ID tag, a leash, a crate or bed, food appropriate for their age, and a few chew-safe toys before they come home. Everything else can be figured out in the first week or two.</p>
<h2 id="day-one" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Day-One Must-Haves</h2>
<p>Food and water bowls (stainless steel or ceramic hold up better than plastic), a properly-fitted collar with an ID tag, a 4-6 foot leash, and the same food they were already eating (switch gradually later if you want to change brands) — these need to be ready before they walk in the door.</p>
<h2 id="sleeping" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Sleeping Setup</h2>
<p>A crate sized so they can stand, turn around, and lie down comfortably (not much bigger — extra room can encourage using one corner as a bathroom) plus a soft bed or blanket inside it. Even if you don't plan to crate train long-term, having one for the first few weeks makes housetraining and vet visits much easier.</p>
<h2 id="cleanup" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Cleanup Supplies</h2>
<p>Poop bags, an enzymatic cleaner for indoor accidents (regular cleaners don't fully remove the scent, which can encourage repeat accidents in the same spot), and a lint roller if you value your clothes.</p>
<h2 id="toys" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Toys &amp; Enrichment</h2>
<p>A couple of chew-safe toys sized appropriately for your dog, plus one puzzle or treat-dispensing toy — see our <a class="cphd-inlinelink" href="/interactive-toys/">interactive toy guide</a> for picking the right one. You don't need a full toy box on day one; you'll learn what they actually like within a week.</p>
<h2 id="grooming" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Basic Grooming</h2>
<p>A brush suited to their coat type, dog-safe shampoo, and nail clippers or a grinder. You can hold off on anything more specialized until you know your dog's specific coat and skin needs.</p>
<h2 id="training" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Training Basics</h2>
<p>Small, soft training treats (easy to eat quickly so training doesn't stall out), and a treat pouch or your pocket works fine to start. A slip lead or standard leash is enough — you don't need specialized training gear right away.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Do I need a harness or a collar?</strong><br/>Both have a place — a collar for ID tags is essential, and a harness is often gentler for walking, especially for puppies or dogs still learning leash manners.</p>
<p><strong>What size crate should I get?</strong><br/>Big enough to stand, turn, and lie down — not more. For a growing puppy, a crate with a removable divider panel lets you size it up as they grow instead of buying multiple crates.</p>
<p><strong>Do I really need pet insurance from day one?</strong><br/>It's worth looking into early, since pre-existing conditions are typically excluded — the earlier you enroll, the more coverage you'll actually have if something comes up.</p>
