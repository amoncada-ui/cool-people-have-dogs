<div style="font-size: 12px; font-weight: 700; letter-spacing: 0.06em; color: #4F6350; margin-bottom: 6px;">QUICK ANSWER</div>
<p style="margin: 0; font-size: 16px; line-height: 1.6;">Yes, dogs can eat blueberries. They're low-calorie, packed with fiber and antioxidants, and one of the few fruits vets actively recommend as a treat. A handful (5-10 berries for a medium dog) is a safe amount — just skip the muffins.</p>
</div>
<p>My dog treats blueberries like they're contraband — the second the bag crinkles, she's sitting at my feet, staring me down. Good news for both of us: unlike grapes or raisins, which are genuinely dangerous, blueberries are one of the safest "people foods" you can hand over. Here's how many, how to serve them, and the one place blueberries actually go wrong.</p>
<h2 id="how-many" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">How Many Blueberries Can My Dog Have?</h2>
<p>The general rule of thumb for any treat, blueberries included, is that it shouldn't make up more than about 10% of your dog's daily calories. In practice, that's roughly 5-10 blueberries for a medium-sized dog, a couple fewer for a small dog, and up to a small handful for a large one. Blueberries are low-calorie as treats go (about 1 calorie per berry), so this is a fairly generous rule compared to something richer like cheese or peanut butter.</p>
<p>If it's your dog's first time trying them, start with just one or two. Blueberries are gentle, but any new food can cause loose stool in a sensitive stomach, and it's easier to notice that with a small test run than after they've inhaled a whole cup.</p>
<h2 id="benefits" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Why Vets Actually Recommend Them</h2>
<p>Blueberries aren't just "not harmful" — they're genuinely good for your dog. They're high in fiber, which helps digestion, and loaded with antioxidants that support your dog's immune system and may help fight cell damage as they age. They're also low in sugar compared to most fruit, which makes them a smarter treat swap than something like banana if your dog needs to watch their weight.</p>
<p>Some vets recommend blueberries specifically for senior dogs, since the antioxidants are thought to support cognitive function — one more reason they've earned a permanent spot in my freezer, not just the fridge.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-serve" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">How to Serve Them Safely</h2>
<p>Blueberries are small and round, which makes them a mild choking risk for tiny dogs or enthusiastic gulpers who don't chew much. If your dog is a small breed or tends to inhale food whole, squish the berries slightly between your fingers before handing them over — it takes two seconds and removes the risk entirely.</p>
<p>Always rinse blueberries first, just like you would for yourself, to rinse off any pesticide residue or dirt. And skip anything beyond the plain berry itself — no sugar, no syrup, no yogurt-covered blueberries from the snack aisle, which usually have chocolate coating hiding underneath (chocolate is toxic to dogs, full stop).</p>
<h2 id="frozen-dried" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">What About Frozen, Dried, or Baked Blueberries?</h2>
<p>Frozen blueberries are one of my dog's favorite hot-weather treats — straight from the freezer, no prep needed, and the cold makes them last a little longer as a distraction. Dried blueberries are fine too, just double-check the label for added sugar, and offer fewer of them since dried fruit is more calorie-dense than fresh.</p>
<p>Baked goods are where blueberries stop being a safe bet. Blueberry muffins, pancakes, and pie filling are usually loaded with added sugar, and some recipes use xylitol as a sugar substitute, which is toxic to dogs even in small amounts. It's the plain berry that's the health food here — not anything it gets baked into.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How many blueberries can I give my dog?</strong><br/>For most dogs, a handful is plenty — think 5-10 berries for a medium-sized dog as an occasional treat, less for small dogs, more for large ones. Treats, including fruit, should stay under about 10% of your dog's daily calories.</p>
<p><strong>Can puppies eat blueberries?</strong><br/>Yes, in small amounts, cut or mashed to reduce any choking risk. Introduce them slowly and watch for any stomach upset the first time.</p>
<p><strong>Are blueberry muffins or blueberry pie safe for dogs?</strong><br/>No — skip anything baked. Muffins and pie filling are loaded with added sugar and sometimes xylitol, which is toxic to dogs. It's the blueberries themselves that are fine, not blueberry desserts.</p>
<p><strong>Can dogs eat frozen or dried blueberries?</strong><br/>Frozen blueberries are a great hot-weather treat, straight from the freezer. Dried blueberries are fine too, but check the label for added sugar first, and offer fewer since they're more calorie-dense per berry.</p>
<p style="margin-top: 32px;">Toss a couple in your dog's bowl tonight and watch how fast they disappear — of everything on the "can my dog eat this" list, blueberries are about as close to a guilt-free yes as it gets.</p>
