faq:
- question: Do all dogs tilt their heads?
  answer: Most do to some degree, though it varies by individual — some dogs are enthusiastic, frequent tilters and others rarely do it at all, which is just personality.
- question: Why does my dog only tilt for certain words?
  answer: Usually words they've learned to associate with something exciting — their name, "walk," "treat" — which makes them lean in for confirmation of what's coming.
- question: Do dogs with floppy ears tilt less?
  answer: Some research suggests breeds with more mobile, upright ears may rely on tilting slightly less since their ears already move independently — but plenty of floppy-eared dogs are enthusiastic tilters too.
- question: Should I encourage the head tilt?
  answer: No need to train for it specifically — it happens naturally in response to interesting sounds, and trying to force it usually just confuses your dog about what you actually want.

<div style="font-size: 12px; font-weight: 700; letter-spacing: 0.06em; color: #B4552F; margin-bottom: 6px;">QUICK ANSWER</div>
<p style="margin: 0; font-size: 16px; line-height: 1.6;">The head tilt is your dog trying to hear and understand you better — it adjusts their ears to pinpoint sound and may also help them see past their own muzzle to read your facial expression. It's a sign of engagement and attention, not confusion.</p>
</div>
<p>Say one word in an even slightly interested voice — "walk," "treat," their name — and my dog's head cocks to one side like she's trying to solve a riddle. It's objectively the most photogenic thing a dog does, and every time it happens I have to resist the urge to narrate a whole internal monologue for her. But there's real reasoning behind it, and it's sweeter than "she's confused."</p>
<h2 id="hearing" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">It Helps Them Pinpoint Sound</h2>
<p>One leading theory is purely mechanical: tilting the head shifts the position of a dog's outer ears, helping them locate the exact source and pitch of a sound more precisely. Dogs' ears are incredibly sensitive to pitch and direction — tilting is essentially a fine-tuning move, like adjusting an antenna, to catch every detail of an interesting new sound.</p>
<h2 id="seeing-you" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">It Might Help Them See Your Face Better</h2>
<p>Dogs with longer muzzles have more of their own snout blocking their lower field of vision. Tilting the head shifts the muzzle out of the way, giving them a clearer view of your mouth and facial expressions — which matters a lot, since dogs read human faces closely for emotional cues, not just words.</p>
<h2 id="recognized-words" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">They've Heard a Word They Recognize</h2>
<p>Dogs who are trained to respond to specific words tend to tilt more often when they hear one of those familiar words — not because they're puzzled, but because they're paying extra-close attention, trying to confirm what's about to happen next. It's essentially your dog leaning in for more information.</p>
<h2 id="tone-of-voice" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Your Tone of Voice Matters</h2>
<p>A higher-pitched, more animated voice tends to trigger more head tilts than a flat, monotone one — which is part of why baby talk to dogs so reliably gets the tilt. It's not that they understand more words in that tone; it's that the pitch and rhythm signal "this is interesting, pay attention," and the tilt follows.</p>
<h2 id="worth-worrying" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Is a Head Tilt Ever a Concern?</h2>
<p>An occasional tilt in response to sound or speech is completely normal. What's worth a vet visit is a head tilt that's constant, doesn't go away, or comes with wobbliness, circling, or a loss of balance — that combination can point to an inner ear infection or a vestibular issue rather than the everyday "I'm listening" tilt.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-family: 'Fredoka', sans-serif; font-weight: 600; font-size: 26px; margin: 36px 0 14px;">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Do all dogs tilt their heads?</strong><br/>Most do to some degree, though it varies by individual — some dogs are enthusiastic, frequent tilters and others rarely do it at all, which is just personality.</p>
<p><strong>Why does my dog only tilt for certain words?</strong><br/>Usually words they've learned to associate with something exciting — their name, "walk," "treat" — which makes them lean in for confirmation of what's coming.</p>
<p><strong>Do dogs with floppy ears tilt less?</strong><br/>Some research suggests breeds with more mobile, upright ears may rely on tilting slightly less since their ears already move independently — but plenty of floppy-eared dogs are enthusiastic tilters too.</p>
<p><strong>Should I encourage the head tilt?</strong><br/>No need to train for it specifically — it happens naturally in response to interesting sounds, and trying to force it usually just confuses your dog about what you actually want.</p>
<p style="margin-top: 32px;">Next time you catch that tilt, know that your dog isn't confused — they're locked in, trying to catch every detail of what you're saying. Which, frankly, is more attention than most humans give each other in a conversation.</p>
